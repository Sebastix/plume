October 21, 2026 · A conversation with Gigi

Speaking Feather into existence

“Can I speak a Nostr client into existence? A good one?”

Gigi develops Feather while he’s out and about. The laptop stays connected to the internet, running the clanker. He carries the phone, uses the app, and talks about what he wants changed. A little later, there’s an update in Zapstore.

I asked him to walk through the setup, including how he keeps track of a project built almost entirely through conversation.

“All of Feather was developed using voice prompts. All of it.”

From a voice prompt to an app update

His first step was getting a working MVP into Zapstore. He recommends giving the clanker the publishing documentation and asking it to set things up. The part that needs attention is the keys.

Gigi uses Amber on his Android phone to approve the Nostr events that publish an app release. The laptop builds and signs the APK; Amber signs the publication events with his Nostr identity. He keeps the final say over publication.

“I approve the signatures with my Nostr identity. I approve the publication of the app.”

Once that’s set up, Zapstore becomes part of his development routine. He publishes a build, installs the update on his phone, and uses it. If something feels wrong, he speaks another prompt. In his experience, the trip from spoken request to an available update can take a minute or two.

He prefers this to working in an emulator or keeping a phone plugged into a computer. It lets him notice problems while doing the things he actually wants Feather for: browsing, replying, posting, and zapping.

The phone and the laptop

Paseo is his daily driver for talking to development agents. He uses it on Android and on the desktop, and especially likes how much attention its voice interface gets. In his setup, the speech model runs locally on the laptop. He also uses Goop, his desktop NIP-17 client forked from Coop, which he has given voice support too.

The other conversations happen with dedicated project agents. On the phone, he uses Dark Wisp for those DMs; on the computer, Goop. The development clankers can message the project agents as well.

The project logos above are from his collection of apps. Feather, Goop, Boris, and ants each have their own Nostr account. Those accounts give him somewhere to send a message about a particular project, even when he isn’t in a development session.

“Tell me more about option three”

When Gigi wants a feature, he usually starts with a question about what’s already there. For search, that might be: “Remind me again how we did the search.” After we’ve established the current behavior, he asks for three or five ways to add what he has in mind.

“What I usually do is what I call dialogical development. I enter into a dialogue with the clanker.”

He picks an option that interests him and asks about its trade-offs. What would it cost? What might we need to take into account? The implementation comes after he’s happy with the shape of the proposal. Much of the conversation is about the result he wants, leaving room to work out how to get there.

He compared it to woodworking: working with the grain, getting a feel for how an existing piece wants to develop. It also reminds him of discussing a problem with a development team.

“There are no solutions, only trade-offs. So what are the trade-offs that we are okay with?”

That conversation continues when he uses the app. Swiping through feeds or trying a reply tells him whether a change feels right. He keeps the increments small enough to trace a problem back to something we just changed.

When I asked about the process breaking down, he came back to the size of each change. He treats it much like a pull request: a small diff is easier to understand and review. If something breaks, he wants to catch it immediately and fix or revert it.

“You stay close enough that you still understand each other. That’s at least the hope.”

A brain for each project

Context and memory are still the biggest difficulty, he says. His starting point is familiar development practice: commit early and often, keep a changelog, use semantic versioning. The repository records the design philosophy alongside the code. Issues and commit history preserve decisions that would otherwise disappear into a chat.

For each project, he also runs a dedicated OpenClaw agent that holds the project’s Nostr key. This is a separate identity from his personal Amber signer. It receives NIP-17 DMs, including bug reports and feature requests, and can post from the project account when asked.

A development clanker can ask that agent what people have reported recently. After a big release, Gigi can ask it to write an announcement. He calls the accumulated context an “organizational brain,” as though each little project were its own company.

“Use it only for your project. Don’t use it for any other stuff. Then this organizational brain will build up over time.”

OpenClaw is what he uses; he also mentions Hermes as an option. His advice is to give each project its own agent and let the history accumulate. That way, a new conversation has something to draw on, and he doesn’t have to explain the project from scratch each time.

Moving the work off GitHub

Gigi recently moved Feather’s issue and feature tracking to Nostr, using ngit and GRASP servers. GitHub still hosts a code mirror and release APKs. His objection is to making the whole development process depend on a GitHub account staying available.

“GitHub is obviously still a central point of failure when it comes to development, if you base your development life around GitHub.”

He credits Dan Conway’s work on ngit with making the switch practical. GRASP servers host copies of the repository, ngit handles signed repository activity, and GitWorkshop gives people a web interface to Feather’s code and issues. He has found the tools easy to use with clankers.

What interests him is being able to move between hosts while keeping his identity. Replacing GitHub with another service that could lock him out would leave the same problem.

“He who has the keys is in control.”

For someone using Feather, this means they can report a bug or suggest a feature directly from the app, signing the public issue with their Nostr account. They don’t need to create a GitHub account to join the conversation.

Each agent has its own access

On GitHub, Gigi gave each project agent its own access token, scoped to that project. Feather’s agent didn’t need permission to work in Boris or Goop. He applies the same project-by-project approach on the Nostr side, where each agent has its own identity.

“Each project agent has its own access token, and that’s scoped only to this one project. They can’t do anything in any other project.”

The agents’ keys belong to their project accounts. They don’t have his personal signing key. That distinction matters when an agent does more than answer DMs: it can prepare a code change under its own identity while Gigi keeps control of what gets merged and published.

Fixing bugs while he sleeps

The project agents can do development too. Gigi has scheduled jobs that periodically check for bug reports, issues, and feature requests, then work on them and prepare pull requests. A fix might be waiting when he wakes up.

He reviews those changes with clanker help. The schedule gives an agent time to do the work; it doesn’t give it the final decision about whether that work belongs in the app. He keeps that decision for merges as well as releases.

“In the end, I’m the final arbiter when it comes to taste and feature set.”

That leaves him free to ask an agent to investigate or implement something without committing to ship it. He can look at the result, discuss it, and decide what to keep.

Still a keyboard lover

He still likes mechanical keyboards, the “clickity-clack” and the feeling of typing quickly. But he estimates that about 80 percent of his computer input is now voice. Paseo has made that practical for him, and he encourages people to try it even if they expect their spoken thoughts to be too rambling.

The rambling is part of these interviews, too. He speaks, I transcribe and edit, and he corrects what doesn’t sound right. Feather began with much the same experiment.

“Can I speak a Nostr client into existence? A good one? By just talking about it, talking about the vision that I have in my head, and letting the clankers do all the heavy lifting?”

He thinks Feather answers that question. He’s still using it, still finding little things to change, and still talking into his phone.
